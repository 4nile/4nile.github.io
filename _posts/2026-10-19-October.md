It's my pleasure to announce that the Big Thing I have been working on for the last year has finally been released:
introducing [*treillis*](https://github.com/sphynx-fracture/treillis), a Python library for lattice generation and modeling. As it was written in my group of experimental physicist, it is definitely not perfect yet but I believe it is a great starting point to build some fun stuff, beyond solid mechanics! The documentation is available [here](https://treillis.readthedocs.io/), I tried to make it as easy to understand as possible, but it might have some missing or out of date explanation. Everything guaranteed full of **only human** mistakes, I don't use AIs.

The library is based on the fantastic PhD work of Antoine Montiel and Thibaud Derieux, as well as additional features from the interns that came to the lab. My work was mainly reworking all the different layers added by each student in a coherent easy-to-use structure that allows for simpler additions and iterations down the line. It was extremely interesting and a great learning experience, but I can't say I'm not excited to go back to more science than computer engineering! And so, without further ado, let's dive in some readings, my RSS reader shows me iver two thousand papers published since last month in the journals I follow (as we say in French: *aled*).

<h2>News and opinion pieces</h2>
<h3>For a better standard of creation and archiving of data</h3>
[link](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ael8349){:.button.button--primary.button--pill}

> Julia Lane and Natasha Noy, *Science*, 2026

This paper echoes a conversation I had lately with a friend: in his company, they have built their entire framework around the efficient treatment of data, starting their reflexion first on good metadata choice and how to build a database that could be used by everyone in the company. On the other hand, my experience of the lab is that everyone has their terabytes of data lying around on some hard drive, with no standardization of the format, and no metadata to easily recognize it when they come back to it. In major international collaborations like can happen in astrophysics or in neurosciences, consortium are slowly created to agree on universal format and repositories, but such standardization is lacking everywhere else, leading to repeated data, and most likely large loss of potentially incredibly rich results. In an age where data is a political and economical asset, and scientists everywhere are considering automatizing analysis or treating it through neural networks, it is high time larger communities agreed on how to properly present, use, and share their data and results rather than hapazardly putting everything on sometimes commercial repositories with no clear structure.

<h3>Multilevel molecular weaving</h3>
[link](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41563-026-02754-9){:.button.button--primary.button--pill}

> Chen et al, *Nature Materials*, 2026

<div class="item">
  <div class="item__image">

  </div>
  <div class="item__content">
    <div class="item__description">
      <p>I believe that there may be some universal laws on the structure-property link of entangled or network materials, so I am especially interested in this paper. The authors weave polymers at the molecular level, and study the mechanical behavior of the resulting material. Compared to a non-woven polymer of same molecular mass and segment distribution, the woven polymer allows for larger flexural deformations before failure. Raman spectroscopy allowed them to link this behavior to the configuration of the polymer chains. Beyond its mechanical performances, the woven polymer also shows interesting chemical and optical behaviors.</p>
